1 · Firebase

The hard blocker

Thirty minutes. The package name is app.hazri — it must match exactly or the app will not find the project.

  1. Create the project at console.firebase.google.com → Add project. Name it hazri. Turn Google Analytics off — the app collects no analytics and the PRD rules out tracking SDKs.
  2. Add an Android app. Package name app.hazri, nickname Hazri. Leave the SHA fingerprint blank for now; nothing in Phase 2 needs it. Download google-services.json when it offers. Send it to me, or drop it at android/app/google-services.json — it is fine in this repository while it stays private.
  3. Authentication → Get started. Enable Anonymous, and enable Email/Password then tick Email link (passwordless sign-in) inside it. Anonymous is what gives every install an account with nothing to type. Email link is the way back to it.
  4. Firestore Database → Create. Start in production mode (I will write the rules), and pick a region close to your users — asia-south1 (Mumbai) or europe-west2 (London). The region cannot be changed later.
  5. App Check → Get started, register the app with the Play Integrity provider, but leave enforcement OFF everywhere. Play Integrity needs a Play Console app, which you have deferred. I will use debug tokens for testing; enforcement gets switched on in Phase 3 when the Play listing exists.
  6. Path B only: Settings → Usage and billing → modify plan → Blaze, then immediately set a budget alert at a figure you are comfortable with.
  7. Add me — Settings → Users and permissions → Add member as Editor, or just send the google-services.json. Either works; the file alone is enough for me to build and wire everything.
Do not turn App Check enforcement on. With no Play Console app the Play Integrity provider cannot issue real tokens, and every request from the app would be rejected. It looks exactly like the backend being broken.

2 · Release signing key

Optional in Phase 2

You asked for the steps, so here they are — but be clear that Phase 2 does not need this. Debug builds sign themselves. It is worth ten minutes only if you want to test a release build, which is the one that shows real start-up time and catches code-shrinking problems.

  1. Create the key. Run this anywhere with a JDK — Android Studio ships one. 
    keytool -genkey -v \
  -keystore ~/hazri-upload.jks \
  -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000 \
  -alias hazri
    It asks for a password twice and for your name and location. Answer honestly; the certificate is public.
  2. Store it where it cannot be lost. A password manager, plus one backup somewhere else. Not in this repository — android/.gitignore already refuses *.jks and key.properties, and I have added the rest.
  3. Tell Gradle about it by creating android/key.properties: 
    storePassword=…
keyPassword=…
keyAlias=hazri
storeFile=/absolute/path/to/hazri-upload.jks
    Git ignores this file. I will wire the release build to read it.
  4. Read the fingerprint when Phase 3 needs it: 
    keytool -list -v -keystore ~/hazri-upload.jks -alias hazri
    Send me the SHA-256 line only. It is public by design. Never the file or the passwords.
In Phase 3, turn on Play App Signing when you create the app in Play Console. Google then holds the real signing key and this one becomes just an upload key — which means losing it is recoverable. Without it, a lost key ends the app's life: no update can ever be published again.

What it costs, and how to stop it running away

The Blaze question

Two separate worries: will it ever bill me, and can it bill me catastrophically. The answers are yes, eventually — and yes, but only if you build the stop yourself, because the thing everyone assumes is a cap is not one.

The bill, by daily active users

Checked against Google's published rates on 19 September 2026: $0.03 per 100,000 reads, $0.09 per 100,000 writes, $0.01 per 100,000 deletes, about $0.15 per GiB-month stored, and outbound transfer free to 10 GiB a month then $0.12 a GiB. The free quota — 50,000 reads, 20,000 writes and 20,000 deletes every day, reset around midnight Pacific — continues once billing is on; the page states outright that the listed prices are "the price beyond the free quota".

Modelled on what Phase 2 actually touches. The banis, recitations, calendar, diary and goals are all local, so the network carries only a profile, group membership, group totals and the globe — about ten reads and four writes per person per day.

₹2,000–5,000 a month buys roughly 110,000 to 250,000 daily active users. A million installs, at the ten to fifteen percent who open an app on a given day, sits comfortably inside that. Even a million people opening it every single day is about ₹20,000 — four times your ceiling, for an app four hundred times bigger than the one that reaches it.
Two caveats on the figures. They are the default us-central1 column; Mumbai and London may differ, and the pricing page only reveals that through a region selector I cannot read. And Google has changed these rates before — re-check them the day you upgrade.

I will build it that way; it is worth knowing why, because the naive version of each of these is the one a tutorial shows you.

A budget alert is not a cap

This is the part that catches people. In Firebase and Google Cloud, a budget alert sends you an email. It does not stop anything, and it can lag by hours. Every runaway bill you have read about had a budget set.

The real stop has to be built, and Google documents it: point the budget at a Pub/Sub topic, subscribe a small Cloud Function to it, and have that function call the Billing API to detach the billing account from the project when spend crosses your line. Paid usage stops within about a minute.

Hazri survives that unusually well. Every bani, every recitation, the calendar and your whole diary are on the phone. If billing is cut, the app keeps working — people carry on reading and marking their hazri, and only the sangat goes quiet until you turn it back on. For most apps this switch is a catastrophe; here it is a bad afternoon.

Read Google's own warning before you rely on it. Detaching billing is blunt — it stops every paid service on the project at once, and Google notes that for some products this can lose data. Set it up, then test it once deliberately, so the first time it fires is not the first time you find out what it does.

The four things that actually keep the number small

Ordered by how much they matter. The budget kill switch is the last line, not the first.

  1. Firestore security rules, deny by default. The API key in google-services.json is not a secret — it ships in every copy of the app. Rules are what stop somebody reading the whole user table with it, and a scrape is both a privacy breach and a bill.
  2. App Check, enforced, once Play Console exists. Proves a request came from the real app rather than a script. This is the one that is off in Phase 2 and must be on before launch.
  3. Aggregates instead of fan-out reads, as above. The single biggest lever, and it is free — it just has to be decided before the data model is written, not after.
  4. maxInstances on every Cloud Function. A function with no ceiling will scale to thousands of parallel copies under load or attack, and bill for all of them. Ten is plenty at your scale.

Hosting on the free plan

Verified

Hosting is included on Spark — it is not a Blaze-only product. From the pricing page: 10 GB stored, 360 MB transferred per day, with custom domain, SSL and multiple sites per project all ticked on the free column. Past those, Blaze charges $0.026 a GB stored and $0.15 a GB transferred.

Text is never the problem; images are. An article's words are kilobytes and fit anywhere. One photograph on it costs thirty times as much and drops the allowance from tens of thousands of readers a day to about a thousand.

Phase 2 uses almost none of this. The only things hosted are assetlinks.json, which makes the verification link open in the app, and a privacy stub — a few kilobytes a day between them. Vichar's sample content ships inside the app, so it costs no transfer at all.

And if it is ever exceeded, the failure is mild: on Blaze a day at ten times the allowance costs about ₹42. This is a quota to design around, not one to fear.

Two things on that page worth knowing

Phone number verification reads "not applicable" on Spark. That does not affect Hazri — the phone field on screen 32 is a profile detail somebody may fill in, not a way to sign in, and there is no sign-in. But it does mean phone OTP is not a fallback available to us on the free plan if the email route ever disappoints.

The Realtime Database row is a different product from Firestore. Its free plan caps simultaneous connections at 100, which would have put a ceiling on the live globe at a hundred people watching. Firestore has no equivalent cap on Spark — its free plan is measured in daily operations instead. That is the reason the design uses Firestore, and it is worth not mixing the two rows up when reading the page.
